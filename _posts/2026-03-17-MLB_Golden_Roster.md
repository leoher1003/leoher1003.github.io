## Project Overview
A popular baseball adage says that "pitching and defense win championships." Modern front offices focus on building a deep 26-man roster, but does depth actually win championships?

This project analyzes 13 seasons of MLB data (2011–2024, excluding the shortened 2020 season). It applies an economic concept, the Gini coefficient, to WAR (Wins Above Replacement) to measure how evenly value is spread across a roster. It then compares champions with other teams on roster-structure metrics (top-3 player reliance and the share of WAR from batters, starting pitchers and relief pitchers), and tests whether a Random Forest classifier can use those metrics to identify champions.

**TL;DR**
- Teams with more evenly distributed WAR win more regular-season games (Gini r = −0.55; top-3 WAR share r = −0.62).
- Champions drew significantly more of their WAR from batters than other teams did (66% vs. 56%, p = 0.005).
- Roster structure alone does not predict who wins the World Series: a Random Forest performed no better than chance out of sample.

## Data & Methodology
- *Data Source:* [FanGraphs](https://www.fangraphs.com/) WAR (fWAR) via the `pybaseball` library.
    - *MLB Standings & Payroll:* [Kaggle MLB Payroll Dataset](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/christophertreasure/mlb-team-payrolls-2011-2024/data)
- *Sample Size:* 389 team-seasons (13 seasons, 13 champions). The 2011 Marlins are missing because the franchise changed its abbreviation from FLA to MIA in 2012.
- *Key Metrics Engineered:*
    1. **WAR Gini Coefficient:** The Gini coefficient is an economic measure of income inequality, where a higher value means more inequality. Here, a high Gini means a team relies on a few superstars. WAR (Wins Above Replacement) measures a player's total value in wins compared to a readily available "replacement-level" player; a team made entirely of replacement-level players is projected to win roughly 29–32% of its games.
    2. **Top 3 WAR Ratio:** The share of total team WAR generated by the team's top 3 players.
    3. **Positional WAR Ratios:** The share of total team WAR coming from Batters, Starting Pitchers (SP) and Relief Pitchers (RP).

## Key Findings & Visualizations

### 1. The regular season rewards depth.
![WAR Gini vs wins](/assets/images/championship_roster_analysis_fixed.png)
Across all 389 team-seasons, WAR Gini is negatively correlated with regular-season wins (r = −0.55, p < 0.001), and Top-3 WAR share even more strongly (r = −0.62, p < 0.001). Teams that spread value across the roster tend to win more over a 162-game season.

Champions, however, span much of the Gini distribution, from the 2nd to the 77th percentile, and the difference between champions and other teams is not statistically significant (p = 0.17). In a short playoff series, a top-heavy roster can still win: the 2012 SF Giants had the highest Gini (0.83) and the highest Top-3 share (0.51) of any champion, and were the only champion above 0.5.

### 2. Where champions get their value: batters
![Positional Balance](/assets/images/positional_scatter_analysis.png)
The Gini coefficient tells us *how* WAR is distributed, not *where* it comes from. Comparing champions with all other teams (Mann-Whitney U test):

| Share of team WAR | Champions | Other teams | p |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Batters | 66% | 56% | 0.005 |
| Starting pitchers | 26% | 34% | 0.012 |
| Relief pitchers | 8% | 10% | 0.17 |

Champions drew significantly more of their WAR from batters and less from starting pitchers. Bullpen share showed no significant difference.

## Machine Learning Check
To see whether these roster-structure metrics can identify champions, I trained a **Random Forest Classifier** on all five metrics.
![Feature Importance](/assets/images/ml_feature_importance.png)

**Addressing Data Imbalance:** Predicting World Series winners is highly imbalanced (13 champions out of 389 team-seasons, about 3.3%). To keep the model from predicting "no" for every team, `class_weight='balanced'` was used to penalize misclassifying the minority class.

**Feature importance:** Trained on the full dataset, the model ranked **Batter WAR Ratio** highest, consistent with the comparison above.

**Out-of-sample check:** I then validated the model with leave-one-season-out cross-validation (train on 12 seasons, predict the held-out season, repeat for all 13).

| Approach | ROC-AUC |
|---|---:|
| Random Forest on roster-structure metrics | 0.52 |
| Regular-season wins alone | 0.87 |

The model did no better than chance at identifying the champion of a season it had not seen, while simple regular-season wins did far better. The feature importances therefore describe this sample; they are not a predictive rule. Roster *structure* alone says little about who wins a short playoff series.

## Conclusion
1. **Depth matters for the 162-game season.**
A low-Gini roster is associated with more regular-season wins, and relying heavily on a few stars is associated with fewer.

2. **Batter WAR share is the clearest difference between champions and other teams.**
Champions got about two-thirds of their WAR from batters. One explanation: position players contribute in every game and in several ways (batting, baserunning and fielding all feed into FanGraphs batter WAR), while each pitcher appears in only a fraction of innings. Note that champions were also stronger teams overall, which may explain part of this difference (see Limitations).

3. **Roster structure does not predict the champion.**
No metric here, alone or combined in a Random Forest, identified champions out of sample. Short-series outcomes are hard to predict from roster construction.


## Limitations

1. **Short-series randomness:** Playoff series are short, so outcomes are hard to predict from season-long WAR.
2. **Small, imbalanced sample:** Each season has 1 champion and 29 non-champions, giving 13 positive cases out of 389 team-seasons (about 3.3%).
3. **Confounding with team strength:** Champions also had far more total WAR (45 vs. 31) and wins (96 vs. 80), so a higher batter share may partly reflect overall team strength rather than roster structure.
4. **No predictive power:** The Random Forest did not generalize to unseen seasons (ROC-AUC 0.52), so its feature importances should not be read as causes of winning.
5. **Unquantified factors:** The analysis ignores things that are hard to measure, such as managerial skill, health in October and clubhouse chemistry.
